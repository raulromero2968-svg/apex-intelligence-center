## Introduction

PSA 9 cards typically trade at 30-50% of PSA 10 equivalent values, meaning PSA 10s command roughly 2x to 3.3x premiums for average cards<sup>1</sup>. Premium multipliers vary dramatically by card: Victor Wembanyama 2023 Prizm Base shows 4.05x ratio ($115 PSA 10 vs $28 PSA 9), Connor McDavid Young Guns exhibits 3.16x ($3,000 vs $950), while extreme cases like 1999 1st Edition Charizard Holo reach 18.75x ($375,000 PSA 10 vs $20,000 PSA 9)<sup>2</sup>.

Michael Jordan 1986 Fleer rookie displays 7.0x premium, Ken Griffey Jr. 1989 Upper Deck shows 11.57x ($2,030 vs $175), and Jerry Rice 1986 Topps rookie hits 38.70x ($87,000 vs $2,245)<sup>3</sup>. The highest price ratios occur when iconic rookie cards face quality control or condition sensitivity issues<sup>4</sup>.

## Method & Data

This analysis aggregates PSA 10 vs PSA 9 price data from JRZY's comparative study, PSA population reports accessed January 2025, sold listings from TCGPlayer and eBay October 2024-January 2025, and grading standards documentation from PSA official sources<sup>5</sup>. Limitations include time-variant premiums (market conditions affect spreads), card-specific factors (population scarcity, player/character popularity), and regional variations in grading premiums.

Modern Pokemon sets (2022-2024) average 48% PSA 10 rates compared to 18% for pre-2016 sets based on population analysis<sup>6</sup>. This creates bifurcated markets where modern 10s trade near raw values plus grading costs, eliminating net premiums<sup>7</sup>. Data covers grading economics including standard service $25-40 per card, bulk $12-18, and express $100-150<sup>8</sup>.

## Findings

The PSA 9 to 10 boundary creates the largest value discontinuity. Base Set Charizard jumps from approximately $2,800 in PSA 9 to $11,500 in PSA 10, representing 311% premium<sup>9</sup>. Lower grade spreads compress: PSA 7 to 8 premiums average 35-50%, while 8 to 9 spreads reach 80-110%<sup>10</sup>. Cards below PSA 7 face liquidity challenges with bid-ask spreads of 20-30%<sup>11</sup>. Centering-sensitive vintage cards show maximum grade variance, with 60-65% of submissions receiving PSA 6-8<sup>12</sup>.

Modern population reports dilute grade premiums. This creates bifurcated markets where modern PSA 10s trade near raw card values plus grading costs<sup>13</sup>. Population scarcity drives outsized returns: cards with PSA 10 populations under 50 examples maintain 12-18% annual appreciation versus 4-7% for populations exceeding 1,000<sup>14</sup>. Japanese exclusive holos benefit from lower submission rates, creating artificial scarcity even in common cards<sup>15</sup>.

BGS Black Label 10 cards achieve 180-340% premiums over PSA 10 equivalents, but liquidity constraints limit institutional accumulation<sup>16</sup>. Raw card discounts range from 35-55% versus graded equivalents when controlling for estimated grade<sup>17</sup>. Discount widens to 60-75% for vintage cards where authentication concerns dominate buyer psychology<sup>18</sup>.

## Implications

Break-even analysis requires $180+ expected premiums to justify standard grading costs of $25-40 per card<sup>19</sup>. Bulk submission tiers reduce per-card costs to $12-18 but introduce 60-90 day turnaround delays<sup>20</sup>. Express service premiums of $100-150 per card only justify for confirmed high-value cards exceeding $1,500 raw estimates<sup>21</sup>.

Modern card investors should recognize population inflation erodes PSA 10 premiums. Focus grading budgets on vintage cards, low-population modern cards, and Japanese exclusives where grade premiums remain robust. Avoid grading modern sets with 48%+ PSA 10 rates unless card values significantly exceed grading costs plus opportunity cost of capital during turnaround periods.
