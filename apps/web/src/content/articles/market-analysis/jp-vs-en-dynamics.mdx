## Introduction

Most Japanese Pokemon cards command higher values than English counterparts due to rarity in English markets<sup>1</sup>. However, no general certainty exists whether Japanese Pokemon cards as a whole are worth more<sup>2</sup>. The Pokemon Company International prints more English cards due to larger market encompassing multiple countries, while Japanese market limits to Japan<sup>3</sup>. Each individual Japanese card is rarer than its English counterpart<sup>4</sup>.

Specific price examples illustrate premiums: common Drifloon from Scarlet & Violet base set sells for $0.04 English via TCGPlayer versus $2.43 Japanese on eBay; Pikachu from Celebrations trades at $0.45 English versus $20.13 Japanese; Stellar Crown's Special Illustration Rare Lacey shows $31.38 English TCGPlayer market price versus $92.55 Japanese eBay listing<sup>5</sup>.

## Method & Data

This analysis aggregates pricing data from Screen Rant comparative studies, Cherry Collectables market analysis, Miraj Trading regional comparisons, Ravaver trade value research, and SNKRDUNK East vs West analysis<sup>6</sup>. Data covers TCGPlayer and eBay sold listings October 2024-January 2025. Limitations include platform-specific pricing (eBay vs TCGPlayer structural differences), shipping cost impacts on international transactions, and temporal price variations based on regional release timing.

Japanese cards often cheaper in Japan but become more expensive overseas due to exclusivity, demand, and shipping<sup>7</sup>. Special Art Rares and Promos in Japanese frequently more valuable than English counterparts, with some being Japan-only releases<sup>8</sup>. Market remains dynamic with values shifting based on trends, availability, and collector demand across regions<sup>9</sup>.

## Findings

For making money selling Pokemon TCG cards, Japanese cards prove far more profitable than English due to greater immunity to price fluctuations<sup>10</sup>. Japanese exclusive sealed releases outperform English counterparts by 12-16% on risk-adjusted basis when controlling for print run size<sup>11</sup>. Tag Team All Stars and Dream League maintain strongest appreciation curves at 28-34% annually for Japanese sealed products<sup>12</sup>.

Korean and Chinese language cards remain undervalued relative to scarcity metrics, presenting asymmetric opportunities<sup>13</sup>. English maintains superior liquidity representing 71% of transaction volume globally<sup>14</sup>. Japanese cards benefit from lower PSA submission rates creating artificial scarcity even in common cards<sup>15</sup>.

Print run dynamics explain premiums. Pokemon Company International serves English market across Americas, Europe, Australia requiring substantially higher production volumes. Japanese market serves single country with concentrated collector base. This structural scarcity creates baseline premium before considering exclusive releases unavailable in English.

## Implications

Portfolio construction should include 15-30% Japanese allocation for growth exposure and scarcity premium capture<sup>16</sup>. Japanese sealed products offer strongest risk-reward profiles given print run constraints and export demand from international collectors. English holdings provide liquidity for capital deployment and exits.

Avoid paying Japanese premiums for cards with English equivalents unless PSA 10 population differentials justify. Focus Japanese allocation on exclusive releases, vintage Japanese-only sets, and Special Art Rares without English prints. Monitor exchange rates as yen fluctuations affect import economics for US-based collectors purchasing direct from Japanese market<sup>17</sup>.
